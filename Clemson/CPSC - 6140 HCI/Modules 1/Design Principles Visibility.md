![[Design Principles Visibility Slides.pdf]]Many design principles that are actually provided in the Donald Norman textbook, The Design of Everyday Things, the revised and expanded edition version published more recently in 2012. Here I have provided some of the excerpts from that textbook just to show you what some common design principles actually look like. One of which is visibility, which is the concept that the controls and the inputs for any system should be explicitly visualized for the user at the interface. So here's an example of a control panel of an elevator. This is from baddesigns.com, which is a website that I frequently go to, to get inspiration and ideas that inspire better design from the bad design examples that are provided there. And so the user might have the experience of pushing a button for that floor that you want to go to. Nothing happens when you do so. You push some other button till nothing happens and then the user is now startled as to what they need to do. So here one of the problems is that the labels for the buttons are separated from the buttons themselves and the labels don't clearly state that this is for a specific floor. Instead the labels themselves are potentially icons and so it's not visible as to what to do. Therefore the control panel unnecessarily obfuscates what the system is actually looking for with regards to inputs and does not explicate what the inputs actually mean and does not clearly visualize the inputs. And then one realizes that you actually need to insert your room card in the slot by the buttons in order to actually get the elevators to work. So how would you make this action more visible? Perhaps make the card reader more obvious, provide an auditory message that says, you know, what to do in the language that the users, the potential users of the system might be familiar with. Provide a big label next to the card reader that flashes when someone enters the elevator stating that the first thing that one needs to do here is to insert their card into the card reader. Make relevant parts visible and make what has to be done quite visible. So here is a way to enhance the visibility of this design. Here's an example of a automatic faucet. So here in this case to turn on you walk up to the sink and then to turn on you walk away from the sink. And it states in a small font towards the bottom of the instruction for this automatic faucet is that black clothing does not operate the faucet. So what do you do if the user is wearing black? So you become invisible to the automatic controls and it can potentially make it difficult for use.
