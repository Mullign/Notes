With regards to these usability goals, there are also some objective measures to operationalize them. For instance, one might consider time to complete a task as an objective measure of efficiency. One might consider the time to learn a task as an objective measure of learnability. And the number of errors that are made when carrying out a task could be learned memorability, and unfamiliar tasks could also be measured. For instance, one could also measure the accuracy of the diagrams that are created by the users or the amount of you know, the characters that are outside the line limitations. When a user is actually writing using a piece of software, using some sort of natural gesture or an input controller or a stylus, you know, those kinds of actions can also be measured. And those errors could also equate to effectiveness and memorability of a task. The usability goals do not assess the overall quality of the user experience, they simply assess specific criteria of the user experience and the overall quality. 

Emotional attributions of the user as part of the user experience are encapsulated with the user experience goals that are highlighted in the following slide. User experience goals can be subdivided into two separate categories. On the desirable characteristics, that could involve things like satisfying, enjoyable, engaging, engrossing, pleasurable, exciting, entertaining, helpful, motivating, challenging, enhancing the perceptions of sociality and sociability, supporting creativity, cognitively somewhat stimulating, fun, provocative, surprising, rewarding, emotionally fulfilling. So challenging and provocative could be taken with in terms of a positive light here, and not really perceived as potentially being negative by the user. Undesirable aspects could be attributes such as boring, frustrating, making one feel guilty whenever one makes a mistake using the software, annoying, somewhat childish, unpleasant, perhaps patronizing. If the software is constantly providing assistance to the user that the user may not need or is unsolicited, the user might find the software to be patronizing. It might make one feel stupid, cutesy, or gimmicky. Usability and user experience goals are selecting terms to convey the person's feelings, emotions, etc. and help designers understand the multifaceted nature of the user experience. So how do usability goals differ from user experience goals? One has to do with performance and efficiency, effectiveness, learnability, specific criteria that have to do with the task that is accomplished by the user with the system, and the other has to do with what sort of feelings, emotions are being elicited by the software from the user when the user is using this particular tool. And are there trade-offs between two kinds of goals? Can a product be both fun as well as safe? This is something that one has to always ask a designer, always has to ask about which particular usability and user experience goals are important. Are there trade-offs between two different kinds of goals? How do we achieve them? How easy is it to measure usability versus user experience? Are other aspects that are still open-ended questions? 

Usability, there are specific usability measuring tools and questionnaires, standardized questionnaires and instruments that are developed by psychologists, human factors researchers, such as the IBM System Usability Survey is one that we will look at later on. And also, for instance, with regard to user experience, one may design specific questionnaires with user experience goals that the designers of the tool could perceive as being important, such as satisfaction, frustration, and engagement, for instance, could be perceived as being the most important, and those could be queried via a custom-designed inventory or a questionnaire.
