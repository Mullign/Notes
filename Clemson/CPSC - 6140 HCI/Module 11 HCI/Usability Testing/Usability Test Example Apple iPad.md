​So here, I'd like to give you an example of a usability testing study. So this was with the ​iPad when it first came out. Usability specialists from the Nielsen Norman group conducted a usability ​test of the websites and the app specific to the iPad. They wanted to understand how the ​interactions with the device affected people, and to get feedback to their clients and developers, ​as well as those who are eager to know if the iPad lived up to its expectations and hype, ​which was being reported at the time it came to the market. So they use two methods usability ​testing with a think aloud in which users said what they were doing, and thinking as they did ​it, and an expert review. Here we describe the usability testing they conducted. A key question ​they asked was, are users expectations different for the iPad compared to the iPhone? A previous ​study of the iPhone showed people preferred using apps than browsing the web because the ​latter was slow and cumbersome at the time. Would this be the same for the iPad? Was the screen ​was larger and the web pages were more similar to how they appeared on the laptops or desktop ​computers that most people were used to using? So the Nielsen Norman group also hoped that their ​study would be able to address the question that many companies were considering at the time, ​whether it was worth developing specific websites for the iPad, and the way some companies were ​doing for smartphones, or would the desktop versions be acceptable when interacting with ​using the iPad multi touch interface. So the usability testing was carried out in two cities ​in the United States, Fremont, California and Chicago. The test sessions were similar. The ​aim of both was to understand the typical usability issues that people encountered ​when using applications and accessing websites on the iPad. Seven participants were recruited, ​and all were experienced iPhone users who had owned their phones for at least three months, ​and who had used a variety of apps on their phone. So they were familiar with the multi touch ​interface on the iPhone. And of course, the usability testing was to study whether participants ​who knew how to use the iPhone could also transition to using the iPad. So one participant ​was also an iPad owner. One reason for selecting participants who used iPhones was because they ​would have previous experience of using apps and the web with a similar interaction style to the ​iPad using multi touch. So the participants were considered typical users. They varied in age and ​occupation. Two participants were in their 20s. Three were in their 30s. One was in in their 50s ​and one in their 60s. Their occupations were food server, paralegal medical assistant, retired food ​driver, account rep and a homemaker. Three were males and four were females. So before taking ​part, the participants were asked to read and sign an informed consent form for agreeing to the terms ​and conditions of the study. So the form essentially described what the participants would be asked to ​do, the length of time needed for the study, the compensation they would be offered for participating ​the participants right to withdraw from the study at any given time, a promise that the person's ​identity would not be disclosed, and an agreement that the data collected would be confidential. So ​next, they were asked to explore the iPad. And then after which they were asked to perform ​randomly assigned specific tasks on the iPad on various apps. The tasks that the Nielsen Norman ​group chose in the usability testing was very diverse. And so they ranged from finding information ​on websites like Craigslist, or iBook and eBay. Also browsing and reading articles through iBook ​of various ebook articles or on Time magazine, being able to look at articles and pictures ​and legibility issues in examining pictures and reading articles, and also searching for recipes ​and planning a trip on a website such as Kayak. So it was very diverse in nature. And it was ​designed to be diverse to examine a variety of different tasks that typical users might do on ​the iPad. The setup that was used by the usability testing group was essentially a ​a camera that was downward facing to look at what kinds of interactions ensued on the iPad. 

​The iPad also was loaded with software that would automatically record and log all of the ​participants multi-touch interaction with icons on the screen, and the types of tasks that they did ​on the virtual keyboard that was presented on the screen. And also a camera that looked at the ​participants face, such that expressions of the participant and emotions of the participants could ​be detected using computer vision algorithms. And the iPad was situated at a comfortable distance ​from the viewer, such that they could interact with all the icons and everything was essentially ​presented at a comfortable reading distance. The problems that were detected by the usability ​testing team was that accessing the web was deemed to be difficult, and participants had a ​challenging time trying to connect to the internet and also accessing the web through a browser. ​Finding where the browser is seemed to be sometimes difficult, because you can ​scroll from one screen space to another screen space, and if the browser was was located in a ​different screen space to the one that was presented to the participant, sometimes participants did not ​know how to get to the web application itself. And then there was lack of affordance and feedback ​with the application itself, so virtual affordance and immediate feedback were sometimes missing. ​Sometimes the app seemed to get lost and or seems to be difficult to find. 

​Knowing where to tap was sometimes an issue. And the actions were by evaluators was essentially ​the results were reported to developers. They were also made available to the public ​through the Nielsen Norman Group's analysis on their website. And accessibility for all users ​was deemed to be an important and something that the iPad design team actively worked on.
