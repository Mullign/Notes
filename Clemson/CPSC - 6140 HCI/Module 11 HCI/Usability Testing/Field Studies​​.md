​So now we have talked about two different types of formal methods of conducting studies ​so far, the usability testing scenario, and the experiments for research. ​Now a third kind of study type that we're going to be talking about is field studies ​or studies that are done in the wild. ​And it's a term for prototypes being used freely in natural settings and then studied ​in that context. ​For example, if there's an app that's supposed to inform users about the items in a museum ​and how to navigate through the museum, you might study a population of users that actually ​go to a museum and are using the app versus another population of users that go to a museum ​and do not have the app to see what is the value added in that experience when there's ​an app to explore the museum. ​So the aim is to understand what users do naturally and how technology impacts them. ​So field studies are often used in product design to identify opportunities for new technology, ​determine design requirements, decide how to best introduce new technology, and evaluate ​technology in use in its natural setting. ​So field studies can range in time from just a few minutes to a period of several months ​or even years. 

​Data is collected primarily by observing and interviewing people, collecting video, audio, ​and field notes to record what occurs in the chosen setting. ​In addition, participants may be asked to fill out paper-based or electronic diaries, ​which run on cell phones or other handheld devices at particular points during the day, ​such as when they are interrupted during their ongoing activity, or when they encounter a ​problem when interacting with a product, or when they are in a particular location. ​So this kind of context-aware field data collection, an example of which is shown here. ​So the context-aware experience sampling tool on this mobile device actually collects ​this kind of data when the participant may be asked to fill out this experience diary ​at particular points, such as when they are interrupted during an ongoing activity or ​when they encounter a problem when they are interacting with this product. ​So the data regarding participants' impressions can be collected then and there in the context ​of when the data actually matters, and it's collected using the smartphone app. ​Another early example of an in-the-wild study was the UBFIT Garden Project app, which evaluated ​activity sensing in the wild to address the growing problem of people's sedentary lifestyles. ​A mobile device was designed to encourage physical activity that used on-body sensing ​and activity inferencing. 

​So the personal mobile device had three components, a fitness device that monitored different ​types of fitness activities, an interactive application that kept detailed information, ​including a journal of the individual's activities, and a glanceable display that ran on cell ​phones. ​So the system worked by inferring what the wearer was doing in terms of walking, running, ​cycling, and using gym equipment based on data collected from accelerometers and barometric ​sensors. ​The sensor data was processed and then communicated to the cell phone using Bluetooth. ​The data was analyzed and used as input for a glanceable display that depicted the UBFIT ​Garden. ​The display depicted a garden that bloomed throughout the week as the user carried out ​the various physical activities. ​The more physical activities, the more flowers bloomed in the garden. ​The healthy regimen of physical exercise was indicated by a healthy garden full of flowers ​and butterflies.
