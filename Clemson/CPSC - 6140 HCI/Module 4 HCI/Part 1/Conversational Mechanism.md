One may want to facilitate these kinds of conversational mechanisms, rules, and etiquettes when we are trying to develop tools for conversations remotely or simulated face-to-face settings. Sax et al noted three specific rules that are quite common to social dialogue in the real-world setting. Rule 1 is that the current speaker chooses the next speaker, and they often do so by asking for an opinion or a question or a request. This is how potentially a conversation might be initiated. Rule 2 is that another person decides to start speaking, so they could do so in responding to a request for an opinion, question, or what have you, or they may just start speaking on their own due to potentially a lull in the conversation, or they may have an opinion that they would like to share. This could happen regardless of someone else's talking, or if there's actually a quiet time in which another person decides that they want to provide their input. Rule 3 is that the current speaker continues talking. 

The current speaker may continue talking regardless of whether someone else in the group is speaking, or in order to initiate a new topic of conversation, to drive the conversation in a different direction, or perhaps during the time that there is no input added by anyone, and there is a lull in the conversation, then the current speaker may continue talking. These are the common rules that Sax have noted that exists in general conversation. Oftentimes, conversations are coordinated and given coherence through the use of adjacency pairs. Utterances are assumed to come in pairs in which the first part sets up an expectation of what is to come next and directs the way in which what does come next is heard. For example, A may ask a question to B, and B responds appropriately. So A may ask, shall we meet at 8 o'clock? B says, can we make it a little bit later, say 8.30? 

Sometimes adjacency pairs get embedded in each other, so it takes some time for a person to get a reply to their initial request or statement. So A might say, so shall we meet at 8 o'clock? And B says, wow, look at him, and perhaps draws the attention in the conversation or the topic in a different direction. And then A then responds to the direction in which B has taken the conversation by saying, yes, what a funny hairdo. And then B then responds by saying, hmm, can we meet a little bit later, and responds to the initial question posed by A. So sometimes B may do this in order to potentially take some time to think about the response before formulating it, or could have possibly been distracted as well in the midst of this conversation and in the midst of responding to A's question. So for the most part, people are not aware of following conversational mechanisms and would be hard-pressed to articulate how they can carry out a conversation. 

They don't really think about these rules. They just simply follow them. Furthermore, people don't necessarily abide by the rules all the time. They may interrupt each other or talk over each other, even when the current speaker has clearly indicated a desire to hold the floor for the next two minutes to finish an argument. So back-channeling is also used quite commonly to signal to continue and follow. So statements like hmm, hmm, ah, for instance, are used oftentimes in order to signal that there is a continuation in what's being said or there is more to follow. When people often disengage from a conversation, there are certain farewell rituals.
