​As mentioned before, all prototypes involve compromises. ​So, all prototypes involve some form of compromises. For software-based prototyping, maybe there's a slow response. ​There may be sketchy icons, perhaps limited functionality that is what is provided in the prototype. ​And the users might think that this is the extent of the functionality that might exist in a product if they're evaluating these prototypes at that stage. ​However, more is possible. So, two common types of compromises is horizontal, where you might provide a wide range of functions, ​but with very little depth with regard to the quality of the feedback that is produced back to the user. 

​And vertical compromise, which is where you provide a lot of detail only for a few functions, ​but the system may not contain the wide variety of functions that you might intend for the final system to have. ​So, from a horizontal perspective, users might feel like the prototype might be over-designed and has hundreds of functionality, ​but with little depth, with little meaningful response in each of those functionality. ​From a vertical perspective, the users might think that the system is very limited in the type of functionality that it provides. ​However, they might get a lot of feedback from that few functionality that is exposed to the user at that stage. ​Compromises and prototypes mustn't be ignored. ​So, always, it's very clear, it's important to make clear what these compromises are, ​and to understand them from a design perspective for the design team. ​And the final product still needs to be engineered, and that's something that the designers need to be clear about, ​and it also needs to be explicated to the potential users of the system as well.
