​So what are the four basic activities of interaction design? ​This is a cycle that repeats itself with each iteration of a software systems development ​process. ​So for each version, you would repeat this particular cycle. ​So as you create an initial piece of software, and you improve upon it in future iterations ​or versions, you would repeat this cycle, this interaction design cycle. ​So the interaction design philosophy of software engineering is this. ​You first establish requirements for a particular version in an iteration of a software development ​process. ​Then you design multiple alternatives. 

​And these alternatives are not directly implemented, but you design them. ​At this particular stage, you may prototype perhaps all of the design alternatives, or ​perhaps based on a subset of a specific design alternative, or maybe even prototype a single ​system. ​And then after prototyping the system, here is where you engineer the software based on ​the design alternatives. ​And then you evaluate. ​The evaluation could consist of evaluating between the prototypes that represent the ​alternatives in the design to choose which one is the best. ​Or it could be essentially just evaluating among a small subset of the designs that became ​prototypes and between them to see which ones perform the best. ​Or maybe just evaluating just one version of the prototype from the various designs, ​and then perhaps just studying users' reactions to it. 

​So later stages, such a design might actually take place. ​So just like any software engineering lifecycle, there is an interaction design lifecycle model ​that exemplifies a user-centered design approach. ​So here, the interaction design lifecycle model augments other models of software engineering, ​such as the waterfall, spiral model, the RAD models, with a user-centric approach to interaction ​design. ​So it's not meant to be a prescriptive model, but it's meant to be as a general guideline ​to interaction design approach to software engineering. ​The first step in this is establishing requirements. ​So in order to design something to support people performing tasks, we must know who ​our target users are and what kinds of support and interactive products could be useful to ​provide to them in order to accomplish their tasks. ​So understanding these needs is gleaned through data gathering and analysis, which are described ​in Chapter 7, which we saw earlier. 

​The second stage in this process is designing the alternatives. ​Here, conceptual design involves producing the conceptual model for the product, and ​the conceptual model describes an abstraction, sort of an outline, of what people can do ​with the product and what concepts are needed to understand how to interact with it. ​Concrete designs include sort of color, sound, the menu options on the screen, what kind ​of icons to use, and based on these concrete designs, a few alternative conceptual models ​are actually put forth. ​So this is the designing alternatives part. ​Prototyping is the next stage, and the most sensible way for users to evaluate such designs ​is to actually interact with them in perhaps control situations or in the wild, where observations ​can be made of how users are interacting with it. ​And this can be achieved through prototyping. ​And it doesn't necessarily involve software. 

​It could. ​So you may perhaps create beta versions or pre-release versions of this particular software. ​But also paper-based design prototypes are very quick and cheap to build, or perhaps ​PowerPoint or some other design tool are available these days. ​So you can prototype the interactions with the system rather quickly and cheaply than ​it takes to completely software engineer the prototype, and are very effective for identifying ​problems in the early stages of the design. ​And through role-playing with users with those dummy prototypes, you can get a real sense ​of what it would be like to interact with this product. ​And finally, evaluating. ​And this is the process of determining the usability and acceptability of the product ​or design that is measured in terms of usability and user experience criteria. 

​So interaction design requires a high level of user involvement throughout the development, ​and this enhances the chances of an acceptable product being delivered. ​So evaluating the design or prototype can be fed back into further design or might allow ​for the identification of missing requirements that perhaps weren't considered. ​So this iteration is one of the key characteristics of this user-centric approach. ​So evaluating is done with potentially real users of the system. ​And then it causes another requirements-gathering, establishing requirements approach for the ​next iteration of the interaction design process.
