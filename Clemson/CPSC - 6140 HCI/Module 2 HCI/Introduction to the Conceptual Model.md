The conceptual model is essentially the designer's mental model of how they want the users to interact with the system and accomplish the tasks based on the assumptions and claims that were explicated earlier. 

So the conceptual model basically has many kinds and ways of classifying how, for instance, users will interact and use a product. We describe them in terms of core activities and objects, also in terms of interface metaphors. So a conceptual model is instantiated in the interface metaphor. The best conceptual model are those that appear obvious to the user, the operations they support being intuitive to the user. However, sometimes applications can end up being based on very complex conceptual models, especially they are a result of a series of upgrades where more and more functions and ways of doing something are added to the original conceptual model. They become extremely complex. So most interface applications are actually based on well-established conceptual models. I'm going to provide a few examples here. A conceptual model based on the core aspects of the customer experience when at a shopping mall underlies most online shopping websites. So the conceptual model of online shopping websites include things that one might do when they actually go to a shop. 

For example, you have the notion of examining products by clicking on them, by viewing the products from different vantage points. So you can rotate the product and look at it. You can also ask other users, maybe other shoppers, what they think about a particular product. You can put products into shopping carts. It doesn't mean that the user has actually bought those items and they are inserted into a shopping cart. But then later on during checkout, you can then buy all the items in the shopping cart or you can basically toss out some items that you may not need or perhaps buy more of a specific item as you need during checkout. So the whole process of this online shopping experience is really based on the user's day-to-day interactions with a real shopping mall or a real grocery store. 

Other classic examples of interface metaphors that realize a conceptual model is the notion that a desktop metaphor, which is an interface metaphor, realizes this conceptual model of if a user wants to effectively interact with an operating system, then he or she needs to have all the interactions that one might have in an office space. So a desktop might involve where you have multiple draws or folders where you keep multiple different files and potentially you have a garbage can in which files that you may not need or you may want to delete can be tossed into that garbage can, which could be recycled from time to time. And you can also click on any sort of file and you can open it and view it. So the desktop office interaction would be a good interaction model for users of an operating system. It was a claim that was made early on. But in order to realize that claim, the desktop interaction metaphor was developed by Xerox in the 1970s. The World Wide Web has its own interaction metaphor of where you can organize web pages, you can search the web pages, you can browse them one by one, you can see web pages in different tabs, etc. And the original conceptual model for the World Wide Web was created by Tim Berners-Lee in the early 80s. And the spreadsheet essentially also has functions that allow you to create a list of items, associated costs, and you could essentially have math functions that allow users to tally up and total various costs, etc. And the spreadsheet was essentially a conceptual model of providing an accounting system in a manner similar to a accounting logbook. And this was the conceptual model that was used by Brickling and Frankston in the late 70s as they were designing the conceptual model for the spreadsheet.
