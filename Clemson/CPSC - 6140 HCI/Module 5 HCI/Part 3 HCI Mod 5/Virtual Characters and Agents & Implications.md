Believability, in fact, refers to the extent to which the user comes to believe an agent's intentions and personality. Appearance seems to be very important. Our simple cartoon-like characters are more realistic characters that appear to be believable. Resembling the human form makes it more believable. Behavior also seems to be important. How an agent moves, gestures, refers to objects on the screen seems to make it much more believable. Exaggeration of facial expressions and gestures seems to show underlying emotions, such as the animation industry. Of late, with virtual characters, believability has been redefined to include social co-presence or plausibility. Social presence is the extent to which the user perceives the virtual character that they're interacting with is communicating in a social manner. Co-presence is the extent to which you perceive a virtual character is present in the environment with you and it corresponds to agents mere presence in the shared environment of interacting with the system. Plausibility refers to the extent to which the social interaction with a simulated entity or a virtual agent is being perceived as plausible. There's also social plausibility or place plausibility. And with regard to appearance and behavior and the importance of the two, anthropomorphic character appearance, facial expressions, and how their lips move when talking were all considered important interface design concerns. For example, the haptic creature on the right is a robotic device. It actually looks like Tribbles from the original Star Trek that mimics a pet that might sit on your lap, such as a rabbit or a cat. It's made up of a body, two ear-like appendages, a breathing mechanism, a purring mechanism, and a warming element. These enable it to detect the way it strokes and responds accordingly. When it is placed in a human lap and that person strokes its fur, it might consider this as a pleasing interaction and will update its emotional state to reflect happiness. This then triggers quick rhythmic breathing that causes its ribcage to press against the human hand. It also stiffens its ears and makes a gentle purring-like vibration. Note how the rabbit has no eyes, nose, or mouth. These are deliberately left off to see how people respond to the primary sense of touch as part of the interaction design.
