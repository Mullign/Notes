So let's first talk about attention. So attention involves selecting things to concentrate on at a point in time from a mass of stimuli that surrounds us. It actually allows us to focus on information that is relevant to what we are doing at any given time, and it could potentially involve audio and visual sensory information. That helps with attention. So sometimes while we are watching TV, we could also listen to a conversation or if you're at a social gathering, and if you're paying attention to the news broadcast or sports on a television display, then you can also potentially filter out the conversation and the noise that surrounds you in order to pay attention to the media that's playing on TV at a distance. So those are all examples of focused and divided attention. 

So here in this case, attention is divided between viewing an activity on a television while at the same time following along in a conversation as well. It's called divided attention. If you're just focusing on a certain type of stimuli with all your attention, then it can be focused attention. So another example is involving scanning for football results as they appear in the news bar on the TV screen, and attention allows us to focus on the information that is relevant to what we are doing, and this depends on two things. One is having clear goals and objectives, and second, whether the information we need is salient in the environment, whether it's present and whether it captivates our attention and it's there in the environment. So focused and divided attention enables us to be selective in terms of the mass of competing stimuli, but limits our ability to keep a track of all the events. So it's been shown that there's only a few number of things that we could do while we divide our attention and focus on multiple activities, and information at the interface should be structured to captivate users' attention.

So multitasking and attention. The key question is, is it possible to perform multiple tasks without one or more of them being detrimentally affected? Recently, what comes to mind for an example here is, while attending a talk at a conference, I watched a student volunteer in front of me, sort of deftly switching between four ongoing IM message chats on his or her phone, and one at the conference, one at school, one with friends, one at the student's part-time job, and reading, answering, deleting, and planning new messages, and adding these messages to various folders in the student's e-mail accounts, checking and scanning Facebook updates and Twitter feeds, while appearing to listening to the talk and taking some notes. Also, the student was Googling the speaker's background and opening up the speaker's publications in the midst of playing a game of Candy Crush. While I was watching the student's activities, I was amazed and I was actually exhausted just watching this for a few minutes. So it was as if the student volunteer was capable of living in multiple worlds all at the same time while not letting a moment go waste. How much of the talk did the student actually take in? 

It's a very important question that I pondered. We are addicted to mail, IM, instant media, social media, news blocks, and we are diverting our attention every 5-10 minutes as we are performing a task. Oftentimes, I watch developers and academics as they're preparing a presentation or writing code, having a window with potentially their instant message chats, or having a window of their Facebook messages, or having another browser window open with their e-mails. So oftentimes, they may be working on their presentation or coding for 5-10 minutes, and immediately as a message comes in, they'd immediately divert their attention, view the message, and that triggers other social activities, such as checking for Facebook posts. And then by the time that they come back to the work activity, then it's about half an hour later, and then they're able to contribute another 5-10 minutes of their attention to their work, and the same thing starts again. They're essentially distracted by another message that comes in their e-mail. And this happens to a lot of us. 

So in HCI research, depending on the nature of the tasks and how much attention each demands, multitasking on memory and attention is possible to some extent, but it has a detriment to our ability to be able to perform well on each of these tasks. Heavy media multitaskers, such as the example that I provided with the student volunteer, are more prone to being distracted by multiple streams that they are looking at than those who are infrequent multitaskers. Infrequent multitaskers are better at allocating their attention when faced with competing distractions. Heavy media multitaskers are easily distracted and find it difficult to filter irrelevant information. So something that has been noted by researchers, especially Ophir in 2009, compared heavy work versus light multitaskers, he was able to note down these findings. So the design implications for attention are the following. You make the information as salient when it needs attending to. 

Use techniques that makes things stand out, like color, order, spacing, underlining, sequencing, and animation. Avoid clutter at the interface with too much information. This makes it very hard to search and find the information that one is looking for. Search engines and form fill-ins that have simple and clean interfaces are always easy to use.
