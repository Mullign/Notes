Okay, hierarchical clustering. K-means clustering requires pre-specifying the number of clusters. That four guess that we just did was a totally arbitrary number pick, and we validated it by looking at a silhouette score, and we saw, hey, there's some things that could be improved from the negative silhouettes, but overall, most of it was pretty well partitioned out. Hierarchical clustering removes this need to know the number of clusters you want beforehand, and we have two types of hierarchical clustering, from a bottom-up or top-down. If you want to be fancy, you could say agglomerative clustering or divisive clustering, but it's really top-down and bottom-up. Here we have a dendogram showing from left to right a full set, A, B, C, D, E, and moving from left to right, we have split off A, B into our top branch and C, D, E into our bottom. This is hierarchical clustering in a visual form, just going from one extreme of everything all together to another extreme of everything all separate, all apart. 

So that's the idea. The actual algorithm behind it, each observation starts as its own cluster. At each step of the algorithm, two clusters that are most similar are combined into a new larger cluster. This process of combining clusters is repeated until all observations are members of one single large cluster. So when we think about having each pair of points having a distance between each other, we take the two that are nearest at each step and we pull them together until we have everything all together, at which point we have agglomerated everything into partitions. So there are a few ways to do this. This is a category of clustering, complete or maximum linkage clustering. 

For every two clusters, determine the maximum dissimilarity between any observation in the first cluster, any observation in the second cluster. Single linkage, for two clusters, determine the minimum dissimilarity between any observation in the first cluster, any observation in the second cluster. Or average linkage, compute all pairwise similarities between observations in the first and second clusters and calculate the average. These are just ways that we can sort of implement the distance between clusters as we join them together. Depending on the data set that you are working with, one of these may be more probable than the other. That's a decision that you have to make as the data scientist. A very popular method is Ward's method. 

So instead of joining two clusters based on their distances, we use information about the variance of observations within the clusters. At each step, we join two clusters who merge cluster has the smallest within cluster sum of squared distances. It makes a lot more sense with a visual. So we take together all of our sort of nearest. At the very bottom left here, we have Alabama and Louisiana. Joining that together with Georgia and Tennessee next makes the most amount of sense because they are the nearest to each other. So Georgia and Tennessee are right next to each other. 

Alabama and Louisiana are right next to each other. Those two pairs of states, the variation between them is pretty small, so it makes sense to put that together.
