![[Data Exploration Descriptive Statistics Slides.pdf]]Now that we have an understanding about what data actually is, we can talk about how to learn things about it. The data exploration is understanding at a high level the concepts and easy to get at insights into the data, and descriptive statistics are a very intuitive and fast way to do this. The immediate basics of sampling: we have a "population" that we are sampling from, and we have a "sample", the representative subset of objects or events under study. If we think about a population of citizens of a nation, and a sample of citizens that we can ask a question to, that sample is then representing the larger population with whatever questions we ask them to answer. There are lots of considerations when it comes to sampling, especially when we are thinking about sampling human information. A "selection bias": some subjects or records are more likely to be selected. For example, if you ask the people around you what their favorite snack is, there's a selection bias of the people in your location at that time. 

A "nonresponse bias" is when people or records are not easily available, so they are not represented. People who are not in that building at that time are not represented in the sample because they were unable to be found to ask the question to. When our data has a numeric component to it, we often think about the "mean", the set of in-observations of variables denoted x-bar, and is defined as the sum of all the data points divided by the number of data points. This is a typical or center value, but there's still always uncertainty involved when calculating a sample mean to estimate a population mean. It could be that you only found people who were showing one characteristic, so when you use that sample to infer about the larger population, it is inaccurate. To complement our mean, we have a "median". This is the middle value of our ordered set. 

We have a piecewise definition here, x sub n plus 1 over 2 if n is odd, x sub n over 2 plus x sub n plus 1 over 2 over 2 if n is even. This is one of the times that seeing it written out might be a little clearer, so we have a set of ages 17 through 38 here. If we count from the left and from the right one item at a time, we find that 22 and 23 are in the middle of our dataset. It is a even dataset, so we divide by 2 and resolve to a median of 22.5. This can often lead to a alternate understanding of what a typical representative of the dataset looks like. These also don't agree. If we have a perfectly even distribution, the mean and median are the same. 

However, the mean and median not existing in the same points from our dataset indicates to us the skew direction. The mean is sensitive to outliers, while the median is less sensitive to outliers. The distribution is called "right skewed", since the mean is greater than the median, and skewness often refers to following a longer tail. We see on the right-hand side of this data, a longer distribution amount and a wider range from the median to the right side, so we have a right-skewed dataset. How hard in terms of algorithmic complexity is it to calculate the mean? Well, we have to add up all of the values on the datasets and then divide by the sum. So we have n additions and then one division at the end, we have a linear big O of n complexity. 

For the median, at most, we have big O of n or big O of n log n. This depends on if we need to sort the data first, if we need to do the additional work to sort the data before we can find the middle index of it, that adds additional complexity to our algorithm, and that additional complexity needs to be considered when we think about the practicality of actually doing these computations, especially for larger datasets. It doesn't make sense to have a mean or a median of a categorical variable. So while we can have a bunch of ages that are added up and then divided, we can do that with numerical data, having a categorical variable such as eye color, brown, green, blue, how do you add these up and how do you find out what the middle variable are? It does not make sense to have a mean or median. Instead, we use different method to find a "representative" value. We have the types of pets here showing the "mode". 

The most often represented category could be much more useful when talking about categorical data. We have dog here, which is the most represented in this dataset. In addition to the mean, median, and mode, we often think about the "range" of a dataset. This is just the maximum value minus the minimum value. So once again, this does not make sense to apply to a categorical variable, but is a useful piece of information to know about our quantitative data as we figure out what the possibility of options are. The (sample) "variance" denoted S squared measures how much on average the sample values deviate from the mean. So we have awesome looking mathematical equation here. 

Note the term X sub i minus X bar, absolute value of, measures the amount by which each X sub i deviates from the mean, which is X bar, remember. Squaring these deviations means that S squared is sensitive to extreme values also known as "outliers". So notice here that we don't have the same units. Spread does not have the same units as the actual items. So we need to consider what's a really high variance means. If we have a immensely large or immensely low value, what does that mean? As a measure of spread, we can talk about the "standard deviation". 

So this is the standard deviation of a sample denoted S is the square root of the variance. This is the same mathematical equation as before, just with a square root applied to it. Here, S does have the same units as the X sub i.
