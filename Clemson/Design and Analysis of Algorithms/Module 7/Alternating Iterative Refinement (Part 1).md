A large number of iterative refinement algorithms operate by updating a small part of your solution and then maybe holding that small part of the solution fixed and then updating another part of your solution and holding that fixed and going back to the original part and updating it back and forth in alternation. So let's take a look at a few prominent examples of these sorts of alternating iterative refinement algorithms. As a good starting point in the world of optimization we have a simple approach sometimes called coordinate descent where you optimize a function of several variables by basically cycling through the variables one at a time. So you hold every variable except one fixed and then solve a one variable optimization problem to minimize or maximize over a single variable and then you do that for every single variable in succession, cycling through the variables. Sometimes you might pick a random variable in each step to update. Sometimes you randomly choose a variable with probability proportional to how important that variable is, maybe according to its magnitude in the gradient of your function or something. So there are several different variations on this theme. 

Sometimes you update a block of variables at a time, but in general the idea is you don't update the entire solution all at once. You kind of update little pieces of the solution in alternation holding the rest fixed as you do so. A nice example of that in the world of solving linear systems is Gauss-Seidel relaxation. This is an iterative method for solving a linear system of equations and we'll talk in more detail about solving linear systems of equations in the final module, but just to kind of give you a sense of how this works, it is basically just operating by coordinate descent. So you take the first equation and you solve that first equation for an updated value of your first variable. Then you take the second equation, solve it for an updated value of the second variable and so on. A nice picture of what's going on here, at least in two dimensions where solving a linear system is looking for the intersection of two lines, is basically you're snapping a point to the first line by updating the first coordinate, then you snap to the second line by updating the second coordinate and so on as you kind of spiral inwards towards the intersection of those two lines, which is the solution of your linear system. 

You do have to be a bit careful about making sure that your system of equations satisfies the right conditions for this to actually converge. If you're not careful, then this can actually spiral outwards instead of spiraling inwards and we'll talk a little bit more about that in the final module. So linear systems, as we know, usually take n cubed time or slightly less to solve using either Gaussian elimination or some of the fancy divide and conquer algorithms that we talked about in our divide and conquer module like Strassen's algorithm. So to exactly solve a linear system, an n by n linear system, the running time can be quite intense and that doesn't work very well for the very large linear systems that we often find in the real world. However, very large linear systems also tend to be typically very sparse. They have only a few non-zero entries if you look at the matrix that encodes the linear system.
