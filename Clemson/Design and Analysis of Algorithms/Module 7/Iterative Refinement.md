In this video, we discuss iterative refinement, the basis for a very broad range of heuristic methods designed to give us reasonably good solutions to optimization problems, particularly hard problems that might otherwise be intractable to solve optimally. At its core, iterative refinement is a very simple idea. You just start with some arbitrary solution and keep improving it over a series of iterations. Usually what you do is you look at your current solution and you try and search some small neighborhood around that solution to see if any nearby solutions are any better. Maybe nearby solutions defined by making a small modification to your current solution. Eventually, over a series of iterations, you'll finally reach a locally optimal solution that can't be improved any further by moving to a neighboring solution. This sometimes results in a locally optimal solution that is not globally optimal. 

That's one of the main challenges with iterative refinement is getting stuck in a locally optimal solution. There are different ways of mitigating this, as we'll see in our discussion here. One of them, for example, might be just rerunning the algorithm from different random starting points in hopes that you find better and locally optimal solutions. Let's maybe have a look at iterative refinement applied to a couple of example problems in discrete optimization. We can also talk about some important design decisions that we have to make along the way when we formulate these types of algorithms. In this setting, often, iterative refinement is called local search or neighborhood search because you're searching a small local neighborhood around every solution, trying to find ways of making it better. Maybe if we start with our good friend, the traveling salesman problem, here you have a tour and you're trying to find a way to make a small modification of the tour that maybe improves it by moving to a neighboring tour. 

One way to do that, perhaps, is to take two edges in your current tour and to replace those two edges with their criss-crossed diagonals as shown in the figure here. I guess that would be equivalent to just reversing part of your tour. If you're at City 2, instead of the next cities being 3,4,5,6, now they are 6,5,4,3 in reverse order. This actually brings up an interesting design decision. When you do find a neighboring tour that is shorter, that's better, do you move to that neighboring tour right away, or do you continue searching all possible neighboring tours, and then after that, find the best one and move to it? Both of these could be reasonable alternatives, and you might actually have to run some computational tests to figure out which movement strategy is the ideal one in that case. The same thing would be true maybe in this example here, if you're trying to solve a clustering problem.

Our final example here on the right is from graph optimization. It's the maximum independent set problem. Given a graph, you'd like to find a subset of its nodes that are all independent of each other. No two can be connected by an edge, and you'd like to find the largest such subset. This type of problem shows up a lot in parallel computation. If you have a lot of jobs that you'd like to run in parallel, but some of them conflict with each other because they might make use of the same resource, for example. You can't run any two conflicting jobs at the same time.

Another important idea in iterative refinement is adding a bit of randomness to the mix, and that usually shows up in a technique called simulated annealing. Here, the idea is basically, I have a solution that I like to refine. I look in a neighborhood around me, and if I find a better solution, great, I can move to that better solution. But what if I only find worse solutions? If I find a worse solution, and it's only slightly worse, then maybe I still take a chance and move there. Simulated annealing lets me do that. Basically, if I consider moving to a worse solution, I do so with a probability like here e to the minus Delta over t where Delta tells me how much worse the solution is and t is what's called by current temperature. 

The temperature is set very high at the beginning of your algorithm, and as the algorithm continues to run, the temperature cools and keeps decreasing, eventually reaching zero. When the temperature is very high, then this probability is basically one. That means you're basically just doing a random walk through the state space of all possible solutions without regard to objective value whatsoever. You can just step arbitrarily from solution to solution. As the temperature cools, this approach becomes more like a straightforward iterative refinement approach where you're only allowed to move to better solutions and the probability of moving to a worse solution becomes more minuscule. This approach gives you a little bit more flexibility in terms of your movement around nearby points. It helps with the issue of getting stuck in a local minimum because it gives you a probabilistic chance of escaping from being stuck in a locally minimal solution. 

I guess the motivation here in the name simulated annealing comes from the idea that if you have a molten metal, and you cool it down. If you cool it down nice and slowly, it forms a nice smooth shape. If you cool it down too quickly, you'll get jagged edges and things. Simulated and annealing behaves the same way. If your cooling schedule is too abrupt, then your solution will generally not be so good because you'll get stuck right away in a locally optimal solution, not great. But if you cool slowly, then you have more of a chance to explore in this more fluid manner, your state space of all possible solutions with hopes of wandering into a region that leads you to a better locally optimal solution at the end of the day. A fairly prominent solution approach, it's also nice to know about because this is actually very closely connected with the underlying mathematics that's used in modern diffusion-based methods in Generative AI, which we'll also talk about a bit later in the class when we talk about continuous optimization. 

Iterative refinement is commonly used not only to refine a single solution over a sequence of iterations, but also an entire population of solutions. Perhaps the most famous example here would be genetic algorithms, which are inspired by the way, natural selection in biology improves the fitness of organisms over time to adapt to their environments. How do genetic algorithms work? You start with an initial population, and from there, you build another generation of that population then another generation, and so on. Each generation is constructed using some very simple rules. Each member of the next generation, you obtain by one of several different possibilities. One option is you can just take an individual from the prior generation and mutate that individual. 

This is basically the same approach we've already talked about with neighborhood search. You take an individual from the prior population and then just move it to a neighboring solution with a small change. Another option, and this is really only possible in a population-based approach is you take two of the individuals from the prior generation and mate them together in a very problem-specific way to get an offspring solution that you hope would inherit favorable qualities from both of the two parents. There are several different ways you can do this. Sometimes this is called a crossover operation, modeling how crossover in DNA works. Just to give an example, suppose your solutions are simply encoded as binary strings. This is actually very common in genetic algorithms. 

I have two parent solutions, x and x prime. They're both binary strings. I'd like to somehow mate them together to get an offspring solution that inherits characteristics from both. Maybe the simplest approach here, and this really is a direct analogy to how crossover in biology works is the child solution will inherit part of one parent solution up to a certain randomly chosen crossover point and then switch over to inherit the rest of the binary string from the other parent. That's one very simple approach you could use. But in many problems, you have to think creatively about how crossover should work. If we go back to maybe the traveling salesman problem, how does this work if you have two parent solutions, two tours, and those two tours, they love each other very much. 

How do they generate an offspring tour that would inherit characteristics of both of those two parent tours? There are several ways this can be done. Maybe one approach is, again, you look at your two-parent tours, so x and x prime. They're basically just permutations of your cities. What you could do is inherit directly maybe a prefix of the tour from one parent, and then the rest of the child tour is the remaining cities in the same order that they appear in the other parent. Every problem is different, so you have to think creatively about how crossover makes the most sense for the particular problem at hand. As a silly example that I used in one of my former classes, I asked the students, suppose I have two parent solutions, sardine cookies, and liver, and chocolate chips, and I mate them together, what would the ideal offspring be?

Lots of applications can benefit from that. Then finally, the solutions you get as a result of a genetic algorithm, anecdotally, they're not fragile solutions. They're very robust solutions. They're genetically tolerant. You can mutate one of those solutions and it might still stay pretty good. Whereas, if you look at standard iterative refinement, it might find you one very good solution, but it might be very fragile in the sense that any small mutation might make it much worse. There could be some other good side effects of using something like a genetic algorithm.
