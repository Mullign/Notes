Congratulations on making it to the halfway point of this class, and welcome to Module 6, where we talk about hashing. One of the most important topics in all of algorithmic computer science. What can we expect when we go through this module? We'll spend about the first half of the module talking about hashing in the context of data structures. How to build a so called hash table that implements a set or a map extremely efficiently with all major operations running in only constant time. Hash tables are basically everywhere in practice, definitely a data structure that you want to learn well and one that you'll encounter many times during your career as a computing professional. We'll spend some time also talking about certain types of hash functions called universal hash functions that allow us to prove strong performance guarantees on our hash tables. 

Then finally, we'll look at how we can extend our hash tables to the large scale, looking at implementations of distributed hash tables, and then also to the small scale, looking at data structures designed for limited memory environments that also incorporate hashing in their design. Hashing as a more general algorithm design concept, appears in a very wide range of areas of application, and so we'll spend the second half of our module going through very extensively talking about a very wide range of applications that benefit from hashing. Far too many for me to enumerate at this point, I've listed just a couple on the slide, but we'll spend probably a good hour or more just going over different applications of hashing to showcase how powerful and versatile the technique really is. We'll have a coding discussion where we get to implement hash tables and see how they can actually help us solve simple problems very efficiently. Then finally, we'll have an optional enrichment lecture on a pretty cool topic called locality sensitive hashing, where we actually exploit collisions in hashing for good. I'll talk about some applications of locality sensitive hash functions, possibly even talking about how some of that has inspired a bit of my own research results. This module, we'll also see the first assignment that's not a coding assignment, but rather a paper and pencil style assignment where we have to design, describe, and analyze algorithms in English text, essentially. 

We'll be giving you a handful of problems, and you'll be able to design algorithms for solving those problems, describe how those algorithms work, analyze their performance. All of these are important skills to be able to practice. The goal here is to be able to use the tools and the common algorithms that we've already talked about to put together solutions for how to solve these problems efficiently. Part of your score is based on the efficiency of your algorithms. But part of your score also will be based on the quality of your exposition. How well, how clearly do you describe your algorithms? Being concise is good, but you do also want to make sure that you include appropriate details, the details that matter. 

A lot of times with different algorithms, there are lots of low level details that you can safely ignore. But there are some details that are relatively important that you definitely do want to include. For example, if your algorithm uses divide and conquer, you probably would want to include a recurrence that describes the running time and how you solved that recurrence, for example. This is the first assignment of this type. It usually takes students a little while to get used to this type of assignment. We'll include actually two assignments that are this style of algorithm design, the first assignment here, and then you'll have a chance to get feedback on this assignment, and then you'll do a second assignment knowing the ropes a little bit better. Then when that second assignment is done, you'll have a final exam at the end of the class where the problems will still be of this style. 

But definitely easier on the final exam because that's a bit more time constrained. Here you'll actually have a couple of weeks to think about these problems and to develop your solutions fairly properly. Do make sure that when you write your solutions up, that you use terminology and notation correctly. Part of the goal here is to be able to make sure that we're using mathematics correctly to describe our algorithms accurately using the right terminology. To be as clear as possible, I do ask that you typeset your solutions. Instead of just writing things out by hand, just for one to make it easier for us to read your answers. Definitely feel welcome to include figures, picture is worth 100 words. 

If it helps, include figures. If it helps, include pseudocode, I would shy away from using actual code, but just use a little bit of pseudocode here or there if you think it adds to the clarity of your exposition. But primarily, you're going to be describing your solutions in plain old English prose. You can feel welcome and actually encouraged to work in small collaborative groups. I think you can learn a lot about algorithms by trying to work with other people and explain your solutions to other people. Definitely try and find a study group that you can collaborate with. At the end of the day, though, what you turn in should be your own individual write up, so you can collaborate on the ideas, but then the actual writing itself should be your own. 

Best of luck with this assignment, the course staff will be here, of course, to help you if you need any assistance at all. But hopefully we'll be able to solve some fun problems along the way.
