Lots of applications as well in the domain of security. If you'd like to make sure no one is tampering with your files, you can store hashes of critical files and periodically rehash those files to make sure they haven't changed that nobody's tampered with the files. You can also append a hash. If you're transmitting something over a noisy communication channel, use a hash as a checksum. Append a hash to the end of your message and then the recipient on the other side can verify that the contents of the message when rehashed, they do indeed match the appended checksum. Lots and lots of applications of hashing that you benefit from if you can hash a large object. If we want to consider hashing a large object without loss of generality, we can consider just hashing an array because any large, complicated object can eventually be serialized into an array at the end of the day. 

The fundamental problem we'd like to consider here is given an array of small integers, we would like to hash that into a fairly large integer in some range from zero to M-1, fairly large, but still small enough that it probably fits in one machine word in whatever processor that we're using so that we can easily manipulate these hash values fairly easily and do arithmetic on them in constant time. Here's one proposal. If you'd like to hash an array, then maybe the easiest thing to do is just add up the numbers in the array and take the result modulo M. Modulo M makes sense because we'd like to get an answer in the range zero through M-1. This is a fairly crude hash function. Maybe it's a good idea in some cases. But I would argue that maybe it's not a great idea because if you look at what this does, anytime you permute your array, if you swap two adjacent elements, that won't change the hash of your array. 

You might actually end up with collisions between things that will cause you to think that those things were equal when they weren't. For example, if you take an array and you sort the array, it would still have the same hash as it would in the unsorted version and that might erroneously lead you to conclude that those two arrays were the same when they were not. This hash function is maybe not ideal. You would like small changes in your array like permuting elements to lead to a different hash of the contents of the array. Our go two method for hashing a large array is using what's called a polynomial hash function. Very simple idea, actually. We just take our array of length N and we regard its contents as the coefficients of a large polynomial, I guess of degree N-1, and then we simply evaluate that polynomial at a randomly chosen integer x, which is chosen at the initialization of our algorithm, and then we just use that same value of x throughout the algorithms execution. 

At the very end, we take the result, mod M because that gives us an integer in our target range from zero up to M-1. It turns out that there are several easy ways to actually evaluate this polynomial at of value of x. Maybe one of the most elegant is to rewrite the polynomial according to Horner's rule, which rewrites it in a nested almost recursive fashion. You take a 0+x times a bunch of stuff, which starts with A1+x times a bunch of stuff and so on. If you actually evaluate the polynomial according to this way of writing it, you actually start in the innermost layer. You start with the A(N-1) term, multiply by x, then add the next term, the A(N-2), then multiply by x, and so on. In code, this is ridiculously simple. 

You just keep a running hash value and as you loop backwards over the array, at every step, you take your current hash value times x, and then you add the next term of the array into it. As we go, we typically take the result modulo M after every intermediate step. The way modulo arithmetic works, that's actually equivalent to building up that giant number that would be the evaluation of the entire polynomial and then taking that whole thing modulo M. We don't want to do that because we don't want to have to deal with very large numbers. Oftentimes if we're working modulo M, then after every intermediate step, we'll reduce our answer back modulo M so that all of our numbers stay manageable in size. What is the probability of a collision? It turns out using a polynomial hash function, it's actually easy to do the back of the envelope math that tells us what the likelihood is of two different arrays hashing to the same thing. 

Basically, we would like to know, if I evaluate two different polynomials representing two different arrays, what's the probability that they hash to the same thing when I take the results mod M? That's equivalent to asking, what's the probability that the difference of those two polynomials, mode M gives me zero? Asking if two things are equal, that's equivalent to asking if their difference is zero. I have an equivalent question here of looking at the difference polynomial between P_A and P_B? That's also just a polynomial of degree N-1 where the coefficients are the differences of the coefficients of the A and the B polynomial. The first term is A0-BO. The next term is A1-B1x, and so on. 

I'm interested in what's the probability that I happen to be unlucky enough to pick a value of x that makes this difference polynomial evaluate to zero when taken modulo M. In other words, what's the probability that I've picked a root of that polynomial? A root of a polynomial is a value of x that makes it evaluate to zero. It turns out that if you're familiar with polynomials and how they work over real numbers or complex numbers, a polynomial of degree D can have at most D roots. A line can have at most one root, a place where it cuts the x-axis, a quadratic, a parabolic, and have at most two roots, and so on. Here we have a polynomial of degree N-1. It would be nice if that polynomial had at most N-1 roots. 

The only strange thing about this situation is we're not working over really real numbers or complex numbers, we're actually doing arithmetic in integers modulo M which is a slightly different system of arithmetic. Fortunately, though, it turns out that polynomial root bounds still hold as long as we do arithmetic modulo a prime number. If is a prime number, then our polynomial of degree N-1 will still have at most N-1 roots. There are still at most just N-1 bad values of x out there that will cause this difference polynomial to evaluate to zero modulo M, as long as M is a prime. How many values of x are there that we're choosing between when we pick x randomly? Well, we're working modulo M. All the numbers in the universe are basically the numbers zero through M-1. 

There are M choices for x, N-1 of them are bad, would cause a hashing collision, and so the probability of a collision is at most N-1/M. Back of the envelope, this is really easy to remember. I like to just say that the probability of a collision is upper bounded by N/M, where n is the size of the array, the size of the object you're hashing, essentially, and M is the size of the output range of your hash function. If you're hing a size N object down to a number of size M, then the probability of a collision here is at most N/M. If that probability is too high for you, then you can just change M. You can output to a larger range of possible numbers and you can therefore, drive down the probability of a collision. Of course, if you hash larger and larger objects, that makes the probability even higher. 

This back of the envelope math is very useful in trying to figure out how large a range you should hash to in order to get an acceptable output probability. Let's talk about maybe one prototypical application now of polynomial hashing of arrays, and that's in the world of string matching, a very prominent domain of algorithmic study. The generic problem here typically is you have a very long text and this could be very long, like if you're searching the human genome, maybe several billion characters long. Like to find all the matches of a short pattern within this longer text. There are several ways you can phrase string matching problems. You can say solve a one off problem where I give you a text and a pattern and you tell me all the places in the text where the pattern matches, you can even build a data structure out of the text to facilitate fast pattern matching, where now I do a query based on a pattern and it very quickly tells me all of the matches of the pattern within the text. Lots of ways you can approach string matching. 

But hashing is actually one very prominent way that we effectively can do string matching in a variety of these different applications. One very simple way to use hashing here is you take a hash of the pattern and you compare that to a hash of a sliding window through the text and anytime those hashes match, you surmise that the pattern actually matches that particular sub-string of the text. Of course, you could get false positives here. If the hashes collide, even if the two sub-strings were different, that we already did the back of the envelope math that will help us figure out how large of a range of numbers we need to hash to to drive that probability of false positives down to something that's low enough that we're satisfied that it's not going to be an issue. I guess you could always just verify anytime there's a collision, you can actually go in and just check that the things actually do match just to make sure. The key thing algorithmically here is that if you look at the hash of a window within the text, when you slide that window over, you can actually update the hash in just constant time, which means it only takes linear time to check all of the windows as you slide across the text. Linear time to find all the matches of the pattern within the text. 

Why is it only constant work to update the hash? Well, if you look at the hash of a particular string here, and if you look I've written the polynomial backwards here, so the first value of the text, A0 actually corresponds to the highest order term in the polynomial. I've just written it backwards from the previous direction and we'll see why in a second. But if I evaluate that hash of a particular substring and then I shift the substring over, then what happens? I've lost the first term here and I've gained a new final term, and the power of x has gone up by one on all of the other terms. To update my hash, all I need to do is take the hash from the prior window, subtract off the term that's dropped off, then I multiply everything by x to raise the power of x by one on everything, and then I add in the new final term. All of this you can do in just constant time. 

Maybe I have to pre-compute x^N-1 or something, but it only takes constant time to shift over the window and update its hash accordingly. Now, you'll notice that I wrote the polynomial backwards, and there was a good reason that I did that in the previous slide because since I wrote the polynomial backwards, every single time I shift the window over, that involves a multiplication by x of the former hash value. If I had written the polynomial in the other direction in the forward direction, you'll notice that I would have had to actually divide by x at each step. That's a little bit more of an interesting undertaking given that I'm doing arithmetic in integers modulo a prime number. However, it turns out you can actually do that. That's another interesting feature of doing arithmetic modulo a prime is that you can actually effectively do division. It turns out that in integer arithmetic modulo a prime, every single possible value of x, every integer x, except for zero, has a well defined multiplicative inverse x_-1 that when multiplied by x gives you one modulo this particular M, which is a prime number. 

You can effectively divide by x by multiplying by its multiplicative inverse. Just to give an example of how that might be useful, suppose I have an equation that I like to solve for x. 3x is congruent to four, and I'm working here modulo 7. This is the notation you typically use if you're working modulo some integer, you write congruent two instead of equals. 3x is congruent to four when I work modulo 7, what does that mean x is? Well, normally, if you want to solve that equation, you divide both sides by three. You can do the same thing here. You multiply both sides by the multiplicative inverse of three mod 7 which happens to be five. Because if you multiply 3*5, that's normally 15, but modulo 7, that's actually just one.
