![[Constrained Optimization.pdf]]Oftentimes, when you're solving optimization problems, you aren't optimizing over all space, but instead there are constraints governing which solutions are feasible and which ones are not. So let's take just a few minutes and talk about algorithmic techniques that we can use to help us address optimization problems that have constraints. The general setup here is minimizing an objective function f over some set s that contains all of our feasible solutions. And the difficulty of this problem is going to depend quite crucially on the structure of f and on the structure of s. If you look at the objective function first, probably the easiest case would be minimizing a linear function, and then you would have maybe minimizing a convex function, slightly more difficult, but still relatively tractable. And then finally, minimizing a non-convex function, much, much more difficult. If you look at the structure of s, probably the easiest case there would be if s has a very special structure, like maybe it's just a rectangle or a sphere or something. 

Beyond that, maybe it's described by linear inequality and equality constraints, so it's a convex polyhedron. Beyond that, maybe it's just a convex set. And then beyond that, you might have a non-convex set, much more difficult when you cross the boundary from convex to non-convex. So one special case that we do know how to solve quite effectively is the case of a linear program. When you're optimizing a linear function over a set described by linear equality and inequality constraints, that's a very common special case, and it's very good news if you can formulate whatever problem you're trying to solve as a linear program, because we do have very effective algorithms for handling that case. In fact, even if you're trying to minimize a convex function over a convex set, that is still quite tractable. It's really when you cross the boundary into non-convex territory that things get much more challenging. 

So let's kind of talk about how we can take our existing approaches for continuous optimization, our kind of go-to methods of gradient descent and Newton's method, and adapt them to work with constraints. So all of these methods typically refine our solution by, in each iteration, picking a direction and a step size to move. So what if, for example, movement in that direction takes us out of the feasible set of solutions? How do we deal with that? One very easy approach is to just, after every iteration, we just project ourselves back onto the set of feasible solutions. So you take a step, say, according to gradient descent. And after you take that step, you follow it up with a projection back onto your set of feasible solutions. 

This is called projected gradient descent. And one very nice mathematical feature about this is it turns out that it does not actually impact in a negative way the convergence bounds that we have with gradient descent. And so whatever bounds you can prove based on, say, convexity of your function, those still hold in this case where we're doing projected gradient descent. The only downside is, of course, now there's this extra projection step. And there are certain types of sets S that are easy to project onto, like if S is maybe a sphere or a rectangle. But other sets S might be much more difficult to project onto. And the projection step itself could become quite intractable or computationally heavy. 

So projected gradient descent might be a suitable approach in some cases, but depending on the structure of S, it could end up being quite challenging. So what are some other methods that we can potentially use if projected gradient descent is not on the table as an effective method? Another very prominent method in the case where we have constraints is called the Frank-Wolfe or sometimes the conditional gradient method. And it's also a relatively simple idea. We're basically going to be minimizing a nonlinear objective, but we're going to be doing it step by step. And in each step, we're actually going to minimize a linear objective, which is much easier usually. So what we're going to do is take our current guess, and we make a linear approximation of f around that guess using the gradient of the function at that point. 

And then that gives us a linear objective function. So we kind of linearize our function around f. And then we minimize that linear function over S to find the best possible value of that linear function. Let's call that best possible value y star. And then we just move in the direction of y star according to a standard backtracking line search to find our next guess, x prime. So a couple of steps there, but basically we are solving a linear approximation of our original problem to figure out the direction of movement that we need to move. And then we move in that direction some optimal amount to find our next guess. 

This approach is particularly nice if the set we're optimizing over is specified by linear equality and inequality constraints, because now every single step we take involves a linear program because our objective is now linear. And so this would let us solve a kind of a convex optimization problem as a series of linear programs, which is a lot easier. In this special case, of course, where your set S is described by linear equality and inequality constraints. So this is a fairly popular method and can be used in a wide range of situations. The final approach that I wanted to mention is kind of a high level method. There is a lot of mathematical depth here that I'm going to gloss over, but I think the high level idea is important and it shows up in a lot of different situations. This is sometimes called Lagrangian relaxation, or the general idea of taking a hard constraint and removing it, relaxing it, and turning it into kind of more of a soft constraint that's more of just a penalty in your objective function. 

So suppose you have some problem where you want to minimize your objective function f subject to a constraint that say g of x is less than or equal to zero. What we can do is relax that constraint, kind of remove it so it no longer is a hard constraint but it shows up as now a penalty term in our objective with a coefficient of lambda where lambda is constrained to be non-negative. And because we're trying to minimize, what this does is it basically penalizes infeasible solutions. If a solution is infeasible, that means g of x is going to be positive and that's going to be paired with a positive value of lambda, which adds a positive penalty to our objective and that's bad because we want to minimize overall. So basically we are just penalizing infeasibility but optimizing now over all space, which is much easier than optimizing in the presence of explicit constraints. And so now things get interesting in terms of the question of how do you set lambda? If you set lambda equal to zero, you're effectively ignoring your constraint and just finding the best solution of f over all space. 

On the other hand, if you set lambda to be a value that's extremely large, then basically the constraint is kind of, you know, driving the car in this case, it's all that matters. You are trying to find a solution that is definitely going to be feasible with almost no regard for what f is going to be because the satisfaction of the constraint is what's going to dominate now your objective function. So what you really want is a value of lambda that is kind of somewhere in between that adds enough of a penalty that it forces your solution to be feasible, but not so much of a penalty that the part of your objective involving the penalty term kind of swamps the original objective function f. And you actually, in this case, end up with an interesting duality relationship. This leads in a direction that sometimes gives you something called Lagrangian duality. This function here where you've taken your original objective and you've added to it kind of penalized versions of your constraints, that's called the Lagrangian function, and it shows up in many different situations in continuous optimization. It's a very important entity in continuous optimization. 

And you can actually form an interesting duality result by taking your Lagrangian function. We can give that a name, actually, I'm going to call that d of lambda. So d of lambda is basically the optimal solution you get when you optimize the original version of your problem, but plus that penalized version of the constraint added into the objective function. So minimizing the Lagrangian function over x at a specific value of lambda, that's what I'm going to call the dual function, d of lambda. And it turns out you can show that as a function of lambda, this maximizing d of lambda always is less than or equal to the minimum of your original problem. So you actually have a pair of dual problems, kind of the dual problem is maximizing d of lambda over lambda, and the original problem is, well, the original problem. And so what you end up trying to do is find a value of lambda that maximizes your dual problem here. 

I won't say too much more about this because you can get into quite a bit of mathematical depth here. But what I think I'm going to recommend is as kind of independent reading, you should look up something called the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker, the KKT optimality conditions, because they really characterize in a nice way how you can use this kind of Lagrangian function and these dual variables, these so-called Lagrange multipliers to characterize when you have reached an optimal solution of your problem. And the exact structure of those conditions depends a little bit on the structure of your objective and your constraints and, you know, are they convex, are they linear, that sort of thing. But it is nice. Actually, this is kind of a nice thing to think about with most optimization problems. A first step with solving optimization problems is often identifying conditions that are necessary and sufficient for optimality. Remember that when we solved shortest path problems, we talked about the triangle inequality and how we can use that to characterize when we have reached an optimal solution for a shortest path problem. 

Knowing these sorts of optimality conditions is often useful from an algorithmic perspective because sometimes these optimality conditions can drive the design of our algorithms. A lot of our shortest path algorithms, for example, were driven by the desire to satisfy the triangle inequality, which was our condition for optimality. So knowing kind of how to characterize optimality can be very useful from an algorithmic vantage point. So I would recommend looking up the KKT optimality conditions. And those are useful. It's a little bit more of a mathematical rather than an algorithmic discussion, which is why I think it's probably best just done kind of on your own time as a reading exercise, but useful to know about in terms of how you can characterize optimality for constrained optimization problems. These kind of give you a generalization of the much simpler condition of the gradient being equal to zero in the unconstrained case as a way of characterizing when you've reached a locally optimal solution. 

So that's the final idea that I wanted to mention is kind of this idea of Lagrangian relaxation or kind of turning a hard constraint into a soft constraint. And that kind of leading to this Lagrangian function, which is often useful for not only algorithm design, but also for helping to characterize when you have reached an optimal solution of one of these constrained optimization problems.
